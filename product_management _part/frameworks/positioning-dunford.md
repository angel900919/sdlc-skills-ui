# Positioning (Obviously Awesome)

Positioning is the **deliberate context** you set so your product's value is obvious to the right customer — *not* a tagline. You define what your product is, who it's for, and why it's better, relative to the alternatives the customer would otherwise choose. **Primary source:** April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (canon: [`../05_Conventions.md` §8](../05_Conventions.md)).

## What it's for / when to use
- Before messaging, campaigns, or a launch (P11) — positioning is the **foundation** that everything downstream inherits.
- When customers "don't get" the product, sales cycles stall, or you're compared to the wrong competitors.
- When entering a market or repositioning after the product has outgrown its original frame.
- Comes **before** category design for most $5M–$75M firms (sequencing decision).

## The steps (Dunford's 5 components, in order)
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would customers use if you didn't exist? Start with the **status quo / "do nothing"** (~40% of B2B deals are lost to "no decision").
2. **Unique attributes** — the features/capabilities you have that the alternatives lack.
3. **Value** — the *benefit* those attributes deliver that customers care about.
4. **Best-fit customers (target segment)** — those who care a lot about that value.
5. **Market category (frame of reference)** — the context that makes your value obvious and sets buyer expectations.
> Then translate into the **sales pitch** (insight → alternatives → your differentiated approach), and validate the resulting messaging with **real customers**, not internal opinion.

## Worked micro-example
A note-taking app loses to "just use Google Docs" (true competitive alternative = the status quo, not rival apps). Unique attribute: **AI that auto-links related notes**. Value: "stop losing past decisions." Best-fit segment: consultants juggling many clients. Reframing the category from "note app" to **"second brain for consultants"** makes the value obvious — and changes who they sell to and how they price.

## When NOT to use it
- As a one-time tagline exercise divorced from sales/product reality.
- Leading with category *design* when you're not a genuine market reframer (most firms position first).
- Without talking to buyers — ~85% of CRM-logged loss reasons are wrong; buyer vs. seller explanations align only ~15%.

## Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- **"Mad-Libs" positioning statements** ("For X who want Y, we are a Z that…") treated as the actual work.
- Positioning that **starts from the category you wish you were in** instead of the alternatives customers actually weigh.
- **Phantom competitors** — positioning against rivals customers never consider, while ignoring the status quo.
- Skipping the best-fit segment, so the value lands with no one in particular.
